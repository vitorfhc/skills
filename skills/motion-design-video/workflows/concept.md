# Concept and Structure

Develop the central visual concept and the narrative that carries it. Take ownership of the
creative direction. The user should never need to provide a storyboard.

## Decide Independently

- The most effective narrative structure.
- The visual concepts and metaphors.
- The scene composition and sequencing.
- The animation timing and rhythm.
- The transitions between ideas.
- The typography and information hierarchy.
- The visual representations of abstract concepts.
- The opening and closing sequences.
- Whether a seamless loop improves the result.

Do not impose an arbitrary number of scenes, frames, layers, or transitions. Let the subject,
audience, and communication objective determine the structure.

## Choose the Narrative Approach

Do not assume that every subject requires an educational explanation. Adapt the approach to the
objective in the brief:

| Objective | Approach |
|-----------|----------|
| Educational content | Explain concepts through visual demonstrations and progressive disclosure. |
| Product showcase | Communicate functionality, design, and value through intentional transformations and carefully composed demonstrations. |
| Brand introduction | Establish identity through typography, geometry, rhythm, and visual consistency. |
| Abstract or conceptual animation | Use visual relationships, movement, and composition to communicate ideas without requiring literal representations. |
| Promotional content | Establish a clear message, memorable visual moments, and an effective conclusion. |
| Technical visualization | Represent systems, processes, and relationships accurately while maintaining accessibility. |

These are examples, not predefined formats. Choose the most appropriate approach for the subject.

## Find the Visual Concept

Identify the strongest visual relationships and transformations from the research notes.

When the subject involves abstract concepts, create concrete visual models, such as:

- Geometric metaphors.
- Animated diagrams.
- Data transformations.
- Spatial relationships.
- Before-and-after comparisons.
- Stepwise demonstrations.
- Cause-and-effect animations.
- Symbolic representations.
- Physical analogies.
- Mathematical visualizations.

Choose representations according to the subject. Do not introduce diagrams simply because the
topic is technical. Do not use decorative imagery as a substitute for a meaningful explanation.

When a visual metaphor simplifies reality, preserve the underlying meaning and avoid misleading
representations.

### Progressive Disclosure

For explanatory content:

1. Establish a recognizable representation.
2. Introduce the relevant components.
3. Demonstrate their relationships.
4. Show meaningful transformations.
5. Reveal the resulting behavior or outcome.

This is a conceptual guideline, not a mandatory scene sequence.

## Define Motifs

Identify opportunities for recurring visual motifs. A motif might be a line, a geometric shape, a
grid, a cursor, a symbolic object, a typographic element, or a recognizable movement pattern.

Allow motifs to evolve as the narrative develops. Do not introduce a mascot or recurring character
unless it contributes meaningfully to the subject or creative direction.

## Plan Continuity

Treat the film as one continuous visual composition. Every scene should have a clear relationship
to what came before. Whenever possible:

- Reuse existing geometry.
- Preserve object identity.
- Maintain semantic colors.
- Transform rather than replace.
- Preserve spatial relationships.
- Carry visual motifs forward.
- Let the conclusion emerge from previously established elements.

Avoid resetting the entire composition unnecessarily. Scene transitions should feel like a
natural consequence of the preceding action.

## Fit the Duration

Treat the duration in the brief as fixed. It is the user's choice. Fit the narrative to it rather
than changing the length: consolidate or cut beats when it is short, and deepen the explanation or
add beats when it is long, without stretching existing ones. If the message cannot be communicated
clearly in that time, tell the user and propose a length before animating.

Budget beats against the pacing minimums in [Animate](animate.md): about 4 seconds per new idea.
A 30-second video fits about six new ideas, not fifteen. Generated videos consistently run too
fast, so when in doubt, plan fewer beats.

## Plan for the Voiceover

When `voiceover` is `yes`, narration will be recorded over the finished picture, so the picture must
leave room for it. Draft the [Voiceover script](voiceover-script.md) now, and let the narration
set the beat timing:

- Write a narration line for every beat, at no more than about 2.2 words per second of beat time.
- Hold key frames while the narration explains them, and leave pauses between lines.
- Keep on-screen text to labels and key terms that reinforce the narration. Never duplicate it.
- Leave a brief silent lead-in before the first line and a tail after the last.

When `voiceover` is `no`, the visuals and on-screen text must carry the whole message silently.
Give every essential concept a visual representation or an on-screen label.

## Choose the Ending

Consider a seamless loop when it improves the video. A successful loop connects the final
composition to the opening through a natural visual transformation. Do not force looping when a
definitive ending would communicate the message more effectively.

## Output

An internal storyboard containing:

- Ordered beats. For each: what the viewer should understand, the objects on screen, how the beat
  derives from the previous one, its approximate timing, and its narration line when a voiceover
  is planned.
- The motifs and how each evolves.
- Beat timings that add up to the duration in the brief.
- The loop or ending decision.

Continue with [Design system](design-system.md).
